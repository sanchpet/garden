Thinking

What thinking is and why learn to think

Tags
thinkingdevelopment
Modified
Nov 02, 2026
Reading
14 min

On one of my autumn walks, full of inner monologues about who I am and where I am going, I managed to put into words, quite concisely, the occupation I had given most of my free time to over the past year: developing my thinking. I realised that “learning to think” is an incredibly important thing, one of those big things a life can be devoted to.

When I talk about development, most often I mean first of all the mastery of thinking, and reflection is one of its manifestations.

If I want to devote my life to this, I have to understand what stands behind the simplicity of the phrase. What does it mean “to think”? Why could it matter, and how does one learn it? I wrote this article because I wanted to sort these questions out and be able to explain my answers to other people.

What thinking is

Systems thinking trains the ability to see the difference between a system itself and its function, to split the view of a system into a constructive one and a functional one. To understand more precisely what we mean when we use the concept of thinking, I will try to separate the holder, its ability and the use of that ability.

A person, together with the brain as a bodily part, is the holder of thinking. It is an object of the real world: you can “touch” it, see it and study its structure. Intelligence is a property of this holder.

Intelligence is a person’s ability to learn, to solve problems, to notice new problems, to remember. In practice, to learn means to develop intelligence, and a person’s intelligence shows itself in thinking.

Thinking produces knowledge about objects, properties and relations of the world that cannot be perceived directly. Intelligence relates to thinking as a skill relates to its use. Thinking is work that a person does in the role of a thinker. A person and their brain are physical, while thinking is a process, it cannot be seen or touched. Judgements about how intelligence behaves and how it develops are formed from the products of thinking and from many observed acts of thinking.

An analogy: to get from one point to another, a driver gets into a car. Between the car and setting it in motion stands the mastery of a racing driver. The car is physical, its motion is not. Motion is not constant: it is a process that always happens in a different setting, every ride is unique. But the driver has a trained ability to drive the car competently, smoothly and fast, and with regular practice this ability stays constant and carries over from ride to ride. In this analogy our body and brain are the physical car, intelligence is the driver’s mastery, driving technique is the methods of thinking, and particular rides are acts of thinking.

Why develop thinking

Having defined thinking, we can move on to the hypothesis about developing it. Our time is a limited resource, and every choice of where to invest it is entrepreneurial: it carries risks and potential gains. To convince myself to develop thinking, I need to do the work and find out why developing thinking may be worth its time (or to talk myself out of it, but then there would be nothing to talk about).

Resolving dissatisfactions

A dissatisfaction is a person’s recognised reaction to a problem or an opportunity they have discovered.

In a simple view, resolving a dissatisfaction can be pictured as a sequence. A person thinks, and then takes automatic or deliberate actions.

Our dissatisfactions come from becoming aware of problems. A problem is a recorded gap between the desired state and the actual state, for which there is no clear sequence of actions to close it yet. Once an algorithm for the solution is known, the problem becomes a task. The main hypothesis about developing thinking is that it is thinking that lets a person turn problems into solvable tasks.

More developed thinking turns problems into tasks more efficiently (with less time and/or energy). The results of our thinking are ideas and hypotheses that we can apply to our lives.

Taking part in knowing the world

In this hypothesis I start from the premise that the human brain cannot help knowing the world around it, cannot help absorbing information.

The theory of active inference holds that agents perceive the world and act in it through constant predictions. A person builds a generative model: an inner picture of which hidden causes in the world produce their sensations. Through constant predictions, this model tries to reduce uncertainty and to choose actions that make future sensations more expected or more preferable. When an observation does not match the prediction, a prediction error arises. As a result, the agent can update its beliefs about the world or change its behaviour to get other observations, and this is learning. This explains our curiosity, our thirst for information and for knowing the world around us: this is how we make life more predictable.

Through thinking we come to know the world and attach to our inner network new concepts that make up our picture of the world and that we use to describe the world (or to model it). As said above, this happens mostly by forming knowledge about what cannot be perceived directly.

This is especially visible in growing children: their ratio of information taken in to information already absorbed is much higher than an adult’s, and so their thinking and their models of the world update faster. We notice how a child begins to reason in a new way.

Knowing the world happens whether we do it deliberately or not. But applying developed thinking makes it more precise and faster (more efficient).

Learning applied skills faster

The hypothesis: stronger intelligence, as a result of developed thinking, should speed up learning applied skills. That in turn speeds up resolving many dissatisfactions, raises the chance to stay useful in the world and to have skills one can make a living with.

Throughout human history, social inequality has drawn boundaries. But as information becomes more accessible, these boundaries become easier and easier to cross. Investing time and money in learning a profession that is in demand can radically change the course of a life. This still holds true today, yet people are already starting to feel how shaky the old belief is that one can rely on a single profession all the time.

The closer humanity gets to the so-called singularity, the sharper the pace of change grows. And the faster things change, the more often a person runs into the new and the complex, and the less they can rely on choosing one profession and on the meme “study once, then reap the rewards”. It is hard for me to imagine what exactly will happen to the labour market and to the demand for many professions when AI becomes even more powerful and accessible than it is now. Still, I can be sure that training the skill of dealing with problems I have not solved before will help me cope with the changes better and stay afloat by finding something new to do.

How to develop thinking

Now I will try to move from why developing thinking matters to the methods of developing it.

It is hard to say directly which physical parts of the brain implement intelligence. So this reasoning is more of a plausible model than a measured fact. A shallow answer to the question of how to develop thinking may look like a loop: “to learn to think, you have to think. Thinking develops thinking”.

As we worked out earlier, thinking is the use of intelligence. Through the process of thinking a person comes to know the world and develops. It is important to tell everyday applied thinking apart from the kind of thinking that develops intelligence.

An analogy with training works here: carrying heavy bags home from the shop will hardly grow your muscles; you need to organise training and work with a special technique, with a barbell for example. Just as in the gym we do not start from zero but come already able to lift at least the empty bar, so it is with thinking: intelligence is already there, each of us has been thinking since childhood.

The development of intelligence is easiest to describe as a spiral: today’s intelligence produces thinking that leaves a trace, and tomorrow’s intelligence turns out a little stronger, and its thinking leaves more traces.

So you really can learn thinking only through thinking. But it is thinking of a special kind. To notice the results of your thinking practice, it helps to single out the work products of thinking (concepts, meanings, ideas, hypotheses). This is easiest to do in writing, and then the practice leaves its trace. More on this below.

Conditions for developing intelligence

Novelty of the task. This means running into problems the brain has not met before, for which there is no clear algorithm. Running into the new and the complex seems to grow neural connections.

Holding attention on new concepts. When you reason in writing or out loud, you pay special attention to using the concepts of the relevant theory, to using them correctly and to what they mean. This is the difference from freewriting or small talk, where everyday concepts only run the old picture of the world in circles. When studying a new question, a good practice is to find the best available (SoTA) theory and update your model of the world through its concepts.

This list is not exhaustive for me. Some day I will add to it.

Thinking in writing

I consider reasoning in writing the fundamental way to develop intelligence. As I wrote earlier, thinking practice that relies on work products should make learning more effective. We record hypotheses, ideas or concepts, and these are the results of thinking practice that we can “touch” as proof that thinking work was done. People love seeing the results of their work, and getting any result at all lets you move on with more motivation. Besides, a recorded idea can be reworked and developed in later sessions.

As it happens, writing is currently the most precise way to record the products of thinking. The premise of this reasoning is that thinking with the help of text beats other ways of thinking because it is more precise. A thought or idea you have written down can be read again, corrected, checked for contradictions, worked on further. Writing a text demands more precise wording from the author, and a readable text on a hard topic is painstaking work. Because of this, a person who writes thinks about concepts longer and more precisely. This is the kind of work knowledge is born from.

Working with the garage door open

This video with an excerpt from a talk by Anatoly Levenchuk helped me understand the idea of thinking through writing public notes1.

A thinker writes for themselves. But when they publish a text, they “work with the garage door open”. When you write knowing the text will be published, you demand more of its quality and of the precision of your wording. Publishing a text is a kind of “skin in the game”. Keeping a note “in the drawer”, a thinker risks nothing. Publishing means being ready for your ideas to collide with the real world. When you work in the open, other people can join you, point out mistakes, share their own thoughts.

Once you understand this, you start to pay more attention to what you write, to refine it, to rethink topics you have already worked on. A note on a hard topic means several hours of focus. A topic you were merely interested in and a topic you dared to write a public article about, under your own name, differ in depth tenfold.

And why does this really matter? Because all of this is my work as a thinker. A simple analogy: it is aerobic training, only for the brain. An athlete runs on a schedule, and a thinker trains thinking on a schedule and writes: notes, drafts, sketches. This is a thinker’s physical education, training in modelling. Every paragraph on any topic is a workout. One workout changes nothing, but after some time of writing like this I become smarter. The brain after a year or two of such work is the brain of a completely different person.

Publishing what I write is a nice bonus to the work. Someone will point out my mistakes. And someone may be so taken with what I write about and how I reason that they become a like-minded person. Written culture is what has let humanity move forward. The first task in contributing to this culture is simple: write a text. It does not work right away, but over the years the result will show.

Slow reading and taking notes

A little about the fuel for reasoning in writing, and why taking notes while reading (or while reasoning out loud) is such an important practice.

A planned, systematic intake of good information (as opposed to consuming it at random) should help thoughts and ideas on topics that matter to us come into being. Writing down the main concepts and the thoughts that resonate while reading good literature becomes the first step towards updating your picture of the world, and it starts the creative pipeline.

By picking out, as we read good literature, the thoughts that resonate with our existing picture of the world, we prepare the ground for further reasoning. The value of writing these notes down is the same as the value of writing in general: now you can come back to these ideas, rework them and use them in a draft for reasoning on an important topic. This practice lets you keep valuable thoughts, return to them from time to time, and use spaced repetition to absorb information.

A complex text on a topic that matters to you is very hard to write from scratch, and a coherent argument is built from a collection of such short thoughts, which may visit a thinker over months.

Your answer to the question “How do you think?”

To sum up what I wrote above, I consider it important for myself to be able to answer the question “how do I think?”. This should not be abstract reasoning but a list of specific methods and practices of applying thinking to developing intelligence or to solving applied problems and tasks.

On the other hand, I am always curious to hear from other people how they think. Having an answer to such a hard question that is at least somewhat conscious can give me evidence that a person is aware of how exactly they develop themselves. That is why I decided to leave this part of the note not only for myself but also for a possible reader, who can practise answering this question for themselves with the help of guiding questions.

What I would pay attention to when putting together my own answer to this question:

  • What do I mean by thinking?
  • How do I know that at a given moment I am reasoning?
  • What methods do I reason with?
  • Why does it matter to improve my mastery of reasoning?
  • What methods can I use to develop my thinking?
  • Could I have a conversation with another person about thinking, give advice or learn something myself?

I also decided to leave these questions here so that I can come back to them when I reread this text and think about them, adding to this note through thinking in writing.
